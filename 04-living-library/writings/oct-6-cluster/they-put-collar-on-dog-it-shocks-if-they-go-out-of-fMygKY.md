You're describing an "invisible fence" or "electronic pet containment system," which is a common application of electric fence technology for pets, specifically dogs.  
Here's how these systems work for dogs:

> 1. **Buried Wire or GPS Boundary:**  
   * **Wired Systems:** A thin, insulated wire is buried just a few inches underground around the perimeter of the area where you want to contain your dog (e.g., your yard). This wire is connected to a transmitter, usually located in your garage or basement, which sends a radio signal through the wire.  
   * **Wireless/GPS Systems:** Newer systems, like the Halo Collar, use GPS technology to create a virtual boundary without needing to bury a wire. You define the "fence" in an app on your smartphone.  
> 2. **Receiver Collar:** The dog wears a special collar that has a receiver unit. This receiver is programmed to pick up the signal from the buried wire or the GPS boundary. The collar typically has two small contact points that rest against the dog's neck.  
> 3. **Warning Zone and Correction Zone:**  
   * As the dog approaches the boundary line, the collar emits a **warning sound** (a beep or tone). This is the "warning zone." The idea is to teach the dog to turn back when they hear this sound.  
   * If the dog continues to move closer to or crosses the boundary after the warning, the collar delivers a **static correction** (often referred to as a "shock"). This is the "correction zone."  
> 4. **The "Shock":** It's designed to be a mild, startling, and uncomfortable static pulse, not a painful or harmful electric shock. The intensity is usually adjustable. The goal is to get the dog's attention and deter them from crossing the boundary, not to injure them. Many systems offer different levels of correction, including vibration-only options, so owners can choose what works best for their dog's temperament and training needs.  
> 5. **Training:** Proper training is crucial for these systems to be effective and humane. Owners typically spend time training their dog with flags marking the boundary and reinforcing the idea that the warning tone means "turn back." The static correction is meant to be a last resort if the dog ignores the warning.

**Pros of Invisible Fences:**

> * **Aesthetics:** No visible fence to block views.  
> * **Cost:** Often less expensive than traditional physical fences for large areas.  
> * **Flexibility:** Can be used on properties where traditional fences are impractical or prohibited.  
> * **Portability:** Wireless/GPS systems can be set up in different locations (e.g., at a friend's house or on vacation).

**Cons and Considerations:**

> * **No Physical Barrier:** They keep your dog *in*, but they don't keep other animals or people *out*. This can be a safety concern if stray dogs or other dangers enter your yard.  
> * **Training Dependent:** If not trained properly, dogs may become confused, fearful, or even learn to "run through" the correction (especially if highly motivated by something outside the boundary).  
> * **Stress/Anxiety:** Some dogs may experience stress or anxiety associated with the unpredictable nature of the correction, or they may associate the correction with things in the yard rather than the boundary.  
> * **Inconsistent Correction:** Factors like battery life, thick fur, or even environmental interference can sometimes lead to inconsistent corrections.  
> * **Escape:** A dog motivated enough can often "take the shock" and run through the boundary. Re-entry can also be an issue, as some systems might shock the dog again if they try to come back into the yard from outside the boundary.

So, yes, you're exactly right that these systems use a collar that delivers a "shock" (or static correction) if the dog goes out of bounds.
