The moon's gravitational pull is the primary driver of ocean tides on Earth. It works by creating tidal bulges in the ocean on two sides of the planet.  
Here's a breakdown of how it happens:  
**1\. The Near-Side Bulge:**

> * The moon's gravity pulls on everything on Earth, but its effect is strongest on the side of Earth closest to it.  
> * This stronger pull causes the water in the oceans on the near side to be drawn toward the moon, creating a bulge of water. This bulge is a high tide.

**2\. The Far-Side Bulge:**

> * It might seem counterintuitive, but there's also a bulge on the side of Earth farthest from the moon.  
> * This happens because the moon's gravitational pull is weakest on the far side. The moon pulls on the solid Earth more strongly than it pulls on the water on the far side.  
> * The solid Earth is pulled away from the water, leaving a bulge of water behind. This is also a high tide.

**3\. Low Tides:**

> * The areas between these two bulges experience low tide. The water is drawn away from these regions to form the high-tide bulges.

**4\. Earth's Rotation:**

> * As the Earth rotates on its axis, different parts of the planet pass through these two tidal bulges.  
> * When a location rotates into a bulge, it experiences a high tide. When it rotates out of a bulge and into the area of lower water, it experiences a low tide. This is why most places have two high tides and two low tides each day.

**5\. The Sun's Influence:**

> * The sun also has a gravitational pull on Earth's oceans, though it is about half as strong as the moon's because of its greater distance.  
> * When the sun, moon, and Earth align (during new and full moons), their gravitational forces combine, creating exceptionally high and low tides known as **spring tides**.  
> * When the sun and moon are at right angles to each other (during the first and third quarter moons), their pulls partially cancel each other out, resulting in less extreme tides called **neap tides**.
