It sounds like you're grappling with a really painful and confusing experience in a relationship: the repeated pattern of your partner leaving you alone when you're sad, and how that impacts your connection, self-worth, and ability to trust. This is a very common and deeply damaging dynamic.  
Let's break down why this feels so hurtful and what it implies:

> * **The Core Need in Love:** When you're in love, especially when you're sad, there's a fundamental human need for connection, comfort, and support from your partner. You want them to be a safe harbor, to offer empathy, and to show that your feelings matter to them.  
> * **The Pain of Abandonment (Emotional Neglect):** When a partner consistently withdraws or leaves you alone in your sadness, it feels like abandonment, even if they are physically present elsewhere. This is a form of emotional neglect. It communicates:  
  * "Your sadness is a burden."  
  * "I can't handle your emotions."  
  * "You're on your own with your pain."  
  * "You are not a priority in your distress."  
> * **Erosion of Connection:** Every time this happens, it's like a tiny chip off the bond you share. Trust erodes because you learn, implicitly, that your partner isn't reliable for emotional support when you need it most. This makes it "harder to stay connected" because connection requires mutual vulnerability and responsiveness.  
> * **Dilution of Self-Acceptance:** This is a crucial point. When your partner consistently fails to meet you in your sadness, it can lead you to internalize their behavior. You start to question:  
  * "Am I too much?"  
  * "Is my sadness unlovable?"  
  * "Am I asking for too much?"  
  * "Am I fundamentally flawed if even someone who 'loves' me can't be there?" This process directly attacks your self-acceptance, making you feel like your emotions are wrong or burdensome, rather than valid human experiences.  
> * **"Stupid for believing anyone would become interdependent with me":** This is the ultimate painful conclusion of this cycle. Interdependence in a healthy relationship means relying on each other, sharing burdens, and supporting each other through good times and bad. When your partner consistently fails to do this during your sadness, it shatters your belief that such a reciprocal, supportive connection is possible for you. It reinforces a narrative that you are always alone and that expecting support is naive.

**Why do people do this?**  
There can be many reasons why a partner might withdraw when you're sad, none of which excuse the behavior or negate its impact on you:

> * **Their own discomfort with emotion:** They might have learned that expressing or dealing with sadness is weak, overwhelming, or unacceptable.  
> * **Lack of emotional skills:** They might genuinely not know how to comfort or support someone who is sad.  
> * **Self-preservation:** They might feel overwhelmed by your sadness and retreat as a coping mechanism.  
> * **Emotional unavailability:** They may have an overall pattern of avoiding deep emotional intimacy.  
> * **Unhealthy attachment styles:** They might have an avoidant attachment style, leading them to pull away when closeness or emotional demands increase.  
> * **Manipulation/Control (in unhealthy dynamics):** In some toxic relationships, withholding comfort can be a way to punish, control, or make you feel insecure and dependent.

**What this means for you:**  
Your feelings are not "stupid." They are a rational response to a painful and damaging pattern of emotional neglect. What you're experiencing is the natural consequence of a partner repeatedly failing to provide fundamental emotional support in a loving relationship.  
A healthy, loving, interdependent relationship *requires* both partners to be present for each other's joys and sorrows. When one partner consistently fails to show up for the other's sadness, it fundamentally undermines the security, trust, and connection that love is supposed to provide.
