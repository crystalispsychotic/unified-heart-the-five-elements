It's a common misconception to connect "Newton" and "relativity" in the same way one connects "Einstein" and "relativity."  
**Isaac Newton formulated the Laws of Motion and the Law of Universal Gravitation, which are foundational to classical mechanics.** These laws describe how objects move and interact under the influence of forces, and they work incredibly well for everyday speeds and gravitational fields.  
**Newton's Laws of Motion are:**

> 1. **First Law (Law of Inertia):** An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.  
> 2. **Second Law (F=ma):** The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. Mathematically, this is often expressed as F \= ma (Force \= mass \\times acceleration).  
> 3. **Third Law (Action-Reaction):** For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. (This is the law you initially asked about\!)

**Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation** describes the attractive force between any two objects with mass: F \= G \\frac{m\_1 m\_2}{r^2}, where G is the gravitational constant, m\_1 and m\_2 are the masses of the two objects, and r is the distance between their centers.  
**However, Newton did not formulate "laws of relativity."** The concept of **relativity** in physics is primarily associated with **Albert Einstein**.  
**Einstein's Theories of Relativity:**

> * **Special Relativity (1905):** This theory deals with the relationship between space and time for objects moving at constant velocities relative to each other, especially at very high speeds approaching the speed of light. Its two main postulates are:  
  1. The laws of physics are the same for all observers in uniform motion (inertial frames of reference).  
  2. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the light source or the observer. From these postulates, surprising consequences arise, such as:  
  * **Time dilation:** Moving clocks tick slower than stationary ones.  
  * **Length contraction:** Objects appear shorter in the direction of their motion.  
  * **Mass-energy equivalence:** Expressed by the famous equation E=mc^2, showing that mass and energy are interchangeable.  
> * **General Relativity (1915):** This theory extends special relativity to include gravity and accelerated frames of reference. Instead of viewing gravity as a force, General Relativity describes it as a curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. This curvature dictates how objects (including light) move. It explains phenomena like:  
  * The anomalous orbit of Mercury.  
  * Gravitational lensing (the bending of light around massive objects).  
  * The existence of black holes.  
  * Gravitational waves (ripples in spacetime).

**Key Difference:**  
Newton's laws are an excellent approximation for most everyday situations, where speeds are much less than the speed of light and gravitational fields are not extremely strong. However, they break down in extreme conditions. Einstein's theories of relativity provide a more accurate and comprehensive description of the universe, particularly at high speeds or in strong gravitational fields. Newton's laws can essentially be seen as a special case or approximation of Einstein's theories when dealing with relatively low speeds and weak gravitational fields.
